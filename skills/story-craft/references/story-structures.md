## The three nested time scales

Every piece, from a 6-second bumper to a 12-minute lesson, has three layers. Design all three deliberately:

1. **Macro-arc** — the whole promise. One sentence: "By the end, the viewer will *understand / believe / feel / do* ___." If you can't write it, you don't have a piece yet, you have footage.
2. **Scene beat** — the smallest unit that advances the arc: one claim, one turn, one emotion shift. A beat is not a shot; it's a *change in the viewer's state*.
3. **Frame focal point** — within any frame, the single element the eye should hit first. One dominant focal point, one secondary support, at most one tertiary detail.

The diagnostic: if you cannot state all three in plain language, the design is too busy. This is the cheapest overload-catcher you have — apply it at storyboard, not at final cut.

## Why stories move people: narrative transportation

Narrative transportation (Green & Brock) is the research backbone for *why* story beats persuasion-by-argument in many contexts. When an audience becomes cognitively and emotionally absorbed in a narrative, they are measurably more likely to adopt story-consistent beliefs, attitudes, and behaviors — and they counter-argue less. The levers that produce transportation are concrete: a character to inhabit, a point of view, stakes that matter, and unresolved tension that pulls forward.

The ethical guardrail is congruence: transportation works *only if the story stays true to the real message*. A transported audience that later feels manipulated punishes the brand or the lesson. Use tension and identification to clarify genuine stakes, never to smuggle in a claim the substance doesn't support. (See `production-measurement.md` → Ethics.)

For learning specifically, this pairs with Mayer's **personalization** and **voice** principles: conversational language and a human-sounding narrator induce a sense of partnership and deeper processing. Narration should sound like a competent guide, not a legal disclosure.

## The canonical frameworks

Use these as scaffolding, not straitjackets. Pick the one whose shape matches your objective.

### Campbell — the Hero's Journey
Joseph Campbell's monomyth (*The Hero with a Thousand Faces*): an ordinary protagonist crosses into an unfamiliar world, faces escalating trials, and returns transformed. Disney, Pixar, and Lucasfilm lean on it because it maps cleanly onto identification — the viewer rides the transformation. **Best for:** brand films and longer narrative pieces where the audience should *become* the protagonist (the customer is the hero; the product is the mentor/tool, never the hero itself).

### McKee — Story
Robert McKee's *Story* centers on the **turn**: a scene works when a value at stake shifts from positive to negative (or back). No turn, no scene. The gap between expectation and result is where meaning and emotion live. **Best for:** sharpening any narrative — pressure-test each beat by asking "what value flipped here?" If nothing flipped, the beat is exposition, and exposition is the enemy of pace.

### Pixar — emotional causality and economy
The Pixar approach (and Emma Coats's story rules): character defined by *what they do under pressure*, every beat causally linked ("therefore / but," never "and then"), and ruthless economy — earn your emotion, don't announce it. The "Story Spine" is a fast scaffold:

> Once upon a time… Every day… Until one day… Because of that… Because of that… Until finally… And ever since then…

**Best for:** explainers and short narratives that need to feel inevitable rather than assembled. The "because of that" chain is the test for causal tightness.

### Duarte — the persuasive presentation arc
Nancy Duarte (*Resonate*) frames persuasion as oscillation between **what is** and **what could be**, building toward a "new bliss." The audience is the hero; the presenter is the mentor. **Best for:** pitch videos, vision films, and any piece that argues for change — the contrast structure (current pain ↔ better future) is itself the engine.

## Structure templates by use case

| Use case | Structure | Why it works | Best fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concept explanation | **Question → reveal → mechanism → recap** | Opens a curiosity gap, satisfies it with visible structure, closes with consolidation | TED-Ed–style lessons, onboarding, explainers |
| Behavior change | **Everyday mistake → consequence → easy safer action** | Converts abstract risk into a concrete, repeatable behavior | Safety, health, compliance, PSAs |
| Product / value persuasion | **Hook → problem → product-in-context → proof → CTA** | Matches platform guidance on early branding and directional clarity | Short ads, landing video, sequenced social |
| Brand storytelling | **Small human moment → brand-coded emotional payoff** | Builds connection without over-explaining the product | Upper-funnel brand films, social mini-arcs |
| Interactive learning | **Prompt → attempt → consequence → feedback → retry/advance** | Aligns narrative with retrieval and transfer | Branching modules, scenarios, LMS |
| Multi-audience campaign | **Modular micro-scenes around one central promise** | Lets you re-cut, re-sequence, and localize without re-shooting | Cross-platform, multi-segment campaigns |

### The opening is not negotiable
Across every template, the first beat does outsized work. Curiosity gaps, a character in tension, a concrete stake, or a striking image all work; an abstract claim or a slow context-setup does not. For social, the brand and the core value should be legible in the first ~3 seconds (see `persuasion-platforms.md`). Test openings aggressively — changing the first beat alone has moved real campaign performance materially.

## Narrative economy: cutting to the bone

The strongest pieces are not the ones with the most ideas; they're the ones where nothing can be removed without breaking the arc. Practical cuts:

- **One idea per beat.** If a beat carries two claims, split it or drop one.
- **Kill the throat-clearing.** The first 10% is usually warm-up the audience doesn't need. Start later than feels comfortable.
- **"Therefore / but," not "and then."** If beats only sequence rather than *cause*, the piece feels like a list. Re-link them causally.
- **Earn the emotion.** Don't tell the audience how to feel; build the situation that makes them feel it, then get out of the way.
- **End on the turn or the ask.** Either consolidate (recap/retrieval) or direct (one CTA). Never both, never neither.
